Chances of Becoming an F1 Driver

The honest numbers, and why nobody can give you an exact one.

You will sometimes see answers such as “one in a million” or “0.01%”. They sound convincing and they are mostly invented. There is no global database containing every child who seriously tried to reach Formula 1, and there is not even a clean worldwide count of licensed competitive kart racers.

What can be measured is the funnel. Thousands race karts. A few hundred reach Formula 4. Thirty race in FIA Formula 3. Twenty-two race in Formula 2. And there are 22 full-time Formula 1 seats in the entire world.

Even that last number flatters the odds, because 22 seats do not become available every year. In 2026 only one driver on the grid was a rookie. One year earlier, six drivers began their first full F1 seasons.

The funnel narrows at every level, and at the very end there may not be a seat available when you arrive.

These figures are not directly comparable as conversion percentages. They cover different seasons, drivers can spend several years at one level, and alternative pathways exist. The shape of the funnel is the point, not the arithmetic between rows.

Karting, where the denominator becomes impossible

Almost every modern Formula 1 driver begins in karting, and this is where attempts to calculate an exact probability immediately run into trouble. There is no authoritative published number for all licensed competitive kart racers worldwide.

Licences are issued through national sporting authorities, drivers compete at club, national, regional and international level, countries structure their systems differently, and not every licensed karter is trying to become a professional. The FIA’s own figures give only pieces of the picture: 1,217 drivers from 86 nationalities entered FIA Karting events in 2025, while a year earlier the FIA recorded 629 different drivers in its international championships and more than 1,100 across its International and National Karting Ranking systems. Those populations overlap and none of them is the total.

So a claim like “your chance of going from karting to F1 is exactly 0.04%” is false precision. The denominator is not known. What can be said safely is that the probability for a competitive young karter is well below 1% once the entire worldwide pool is considered, and that this pool is already heavily selected compared with children who simply go karting for fun.

Formula 4, roughly 300 drivers

Formula 4 is where the funnel becomes easier to measure, because it is designed as the first major single-seater step after karting. In 2024 the FIA reported 13 certified F4 championships, 85 events and 300 drivers across its worldwide F4 ecosystem, and it ran 13 certified championships again in 2025 across Europe, Asia and the Americas.

Consider what has already happened to a driver who reaches that point. They have probably spent years karting, beaten large numbers of competitors, found substantial financial backing and shown enough ability to move into cars. And there are still around 300 of them in a single year, of whom a tiny fraction will ever race in Formula 1.

F4 does not lead directly to F3

The ladder is not literally 300 F4 drivers becoming 30 F3 drivers. Many go from F4 to Formula Regional first. Some stay in F4 another season, some move to other championships, and some stop racing entirely. Drivers already in F3 occupy many of the following year’s seats.

Dividing 30 by 300 and concluding that an F4 driver has a 10% chance of reaching F3 would therefore be wrong. There are 30 F3 seats, but they are not 30 fresh vacancies every year, and the true probability of progressing is lower.

Formula 3, thirty seats

By FIA Formula 3 the population has become tiny, with a 30-car grid, and everyone on it is already extremely accomplished. A driver who was exceptional in national karting now races against F4 champions, Formula Regional champions, manufacturer-backed juniors, Red Bull, Ferrari, Mercedes and McLaren academy drivers, and drivers with years of professional coaching behind them. Being very talented is no longer unusual at this level; it is the entry requirement.

Reaching F3 does not make Formula 1 likely either. Since the modern FIA Formula 3 championship began in 2019, Formula 1 says 10 of its graduates had reached F1 by the start of the 2026 season. That is a meaningful number, and it also means the overwhelming majority of drivers who have competed in F3 have not reached Formula 1.

Some may still do so later, and others build successful careers in Formula E, endurance racing, IndyCar or Super Formula. Not reaching F1 does not mean somebody failed as a racing driver. It means the funnel is brutally narrow.

Formula 2, where the odds become measurable

Formula 2 gives the best real-world data on the whole ladder. The grid holds 22 drivers, and because the modern championship began in 2017 there is now almost a decade of history to look at.

Ahead of the 2026 season, 122 different drivers had started an F2 race. Of those, 22 had graduated to Formula 1.

122 → 22 Modern F2 participants who reached Formula 1, roughly 18%

That is probably the most meaningful number in this article. Once a driver has actually raced in Formula 2, roughly one in five in the modern championship’s history had reached Formula 1 by 2026.

It needs three warnings. It is not a lifetime probability, because some recent F2 drivers may still reach F1 in future seasons. The sample is heavily selected, because anybody in F2 has already survived almost the entire funnel. And F2 drivers do not have equal chances: a championship contender backed by an F1 academy is in a dramatically different position from a driver finishing near the back without manufacturer support.

What it does give you is rare. It is an observed conversion rate rather than a guess.

Reaching F2 still guarantees nothing

Imagine getting there. You are one of only 22 drivers in the final development championship below Formula 1. You may have spent more than a decade racing, you may hold the required Super Licence points, you may even win races, and you might still never start a Grand Prix.

The FIA normally requires a new F1 driver to accumulate at least 40 Super Licence points alongside other eligibility requirements, and a top-three finish in Formula 2 is worth 40 points on its own. But a Super Licence only makes a driver eligible. It does not create a seat.

The bottleneck is vacancies, not talent

This is what simple probability calculations miss. There are 22 Formula 1 seats, and Formula 1 does not replace all 22 drivers every year. Max Verstappen does not graduate out of F1 to make room for an F2 driver, and neither do Lando Norris, Charles Leclerc or George Russell simply because another junior has finished the ladder. Successful drivers can hold a seat for a decade or longer.

So the relevant question is not how many F1 seats exist. It is how many become available this year, and that number varies enormously. Six drivers started their first full campaigns in 2025. For 2026 there was one.

A brilliant F2 driver can therefore arrive at exactly the wrong moment. If every team is happy with its line-up, talent cannot manufacture a vacant cockpit.

So what are the actual chances?

The most defensible answer depends on where you start measuring.

Starting pointHonest assessment
Someone who enjoys kartingEssentially impossible to quantify
Serious competitive kart racerFar below 1%
FIA-certified F4 driverProbably low single digits or lower
FIA F3 driverStill a small minority reach F1
FIA F2 driverAbout 18% historically, from the 2017 to 2025 participant pool
F2 champion or leading academy driverMuch better than average, still no guarantee

The first two rows are not precise statistics. They are order-of-magnitude conclusions drawn from the size of the funnel, not tracked cohort probabilities. If somebody tells you a ten-year-old karter has exactly a 0.021% chance, ask where the denominator came from. They almost certainly do not have one.

Why even these numbers miss things

The funnel is not purely meritocratic. Driving ability is essential, and several other factors decide who survives.

Money

Motorsport is extraordinarily expensive, and a driver can be good enough to progress while being unable to fund the next season. Some careers end before anybody discovers how good the driver might eventually have become.

Timing

One driver reaches F2 just as several F1 seats open. Another equally quick driver arrives a year later when nobody is leaving. That difference can decide two careers.

Junior academies

Being attached to an F1 organisation can dramatically increase visibility and opportunity. Arvid Lindblad joined Red Bull’s junior structure young and moved quickly through F4, F3 and F2 before taking the only new seat on the 2026 grid. Academy membership guarantees nothing, and far more academy drivers are dropped than reach the grid, but being in the right programme when a seat opens matters enormously.

The team you sign for

Junior racing is not equal. The team, the engineers, the preparation and the testing programme all matter, and finishing fourth with one team can demonstrate more ability than finishing second somewhere else. F1 teams know this. Fans reading only the championship table often do not.

Luck

Acknowledging luck does not diminish the skill involved. A driver can meet the right team at the right moment, benefit when somebody unexpectedly leaves, get a substitute appearance because another driver is injured, or simply avoid the injuries and funding crises that end other careers. Talent lets somebody exploit an opportunity; luck influences whether the opportunity appears at all.

What if you start karting late?

The odds get harsher. No rule says an F1 driver must begin karting at five or six, but most future F1 drivers accumulate enormous competitive experience while still children.

By the time an elite prospect reaches Formula 4 at 15 or 16, they may already have spent a decade developing braking technique, racecraft, wet-weather skill, overtaking, defending, qualifying pace, setup understanding and the ability to perform under pressure. A talented 16-year-old starting from scratch is not competing against other talented 16-year-olds. They are competing against talented 16-year-olds with ten years of practice.

Is it harder than becoming a professional athlete?

Comparisons are difficult, because sports have different participation numbers and professional structures. Formula 1 does have one feature that makes it uniquely restrictive: the absolute number of jobs is tiny.

There are thousands of professional footballers and hundreds of NBA players. Hundreds of drivers earn money across professional motorsport categories. There are 22 full-time Formula 1 race seats. That does not prove F1 has the lowest statistical probability of any sport, because the starting populations are too different to support the claim. It does mean there is almost no room at the top.

The number worth remembering

For a single figure that captures how difficult Formula 1 is, the place to look is not karting. It is the last step.

Between 2017 and 2025, 122 drivers were quick enough, connected enough and sufficiently funded to start a Formula 2 race. They had already survived almost the entire motorsport pyramid. Twenty-two of them had reached Formula 1 by 2026, about 18%, and that is the conversion rate after getting all the way to the final step. For everyone further down the ladder the odds are dramatically smaller.

The real answer

For a young competitive karter dreaming of Formula 1, far less than 1%. For a driver who has reached Formula 4, still extremely small. For one who has reached Formula 3, already among an extraordinarily small group, with F1 still unlikely. For one who has reached Formula 2, history suggests roughly one in five modern competitors has eventually reached Formula 1 so far.

And even then you need something no statistic can promise, which is the right seat opening at the right moment. Becoming an F1 driver is not a race to be fast enough. It is a decade-long elimination tournament of talent, money, timing, team choices, politics, development and luck.

Chances of becoming an F1 driver FAQ

What percentage of kart racers become F1 drivers?
There is no reliable exact percentage, because no worldwide dataset tracks every licensed kart racer through their career. Judged against the scale of karting compared with the handful of new F1 drivers each year, the rate is clearly well below 1%.
What percentage of F2 drivers reach F1?
Of the 122 drivers who competed in FIA Formula 2 between 2017 and the end of 2025, 22 had reached Formula 1 by 2026, about 18%. Some recent competitors may still get there, so this is an observed historical rate rather than a final probability.
How many Formula 3 drivers are there?
The FIA Formula 3 championship has a 30-car grid.
How many Formula 2 drivers are there?
The Formula 2 grid holds 22 drivers across 11 teams.
How many F1 seats are there?
With Cadillac joining for 2026, the championship runs 11 teams and 22 full-time race seats.
How many new drivers enter F1 each year?
There is no fixed number and it varies enormously. Six drivers began their first full campaigns in 2025, while 2026 had one entirely new rookie.

What if you got your chance?

Reaching Formula 1 is not only about being fast. It is about choosing the right team, knowing when to move up, surviving bad seasons and being ready when a seat suddenly opens.
