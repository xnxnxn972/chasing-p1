How Much Do F1 Drivers Actually Get Paid?

Salaries, bonuses and reserve driver pay, and why the headline number is not what a driver keeps.

A Formula 1 driver can earn anywhere from roughly $500,000 a year to $70 million in team salary, before performance bonuses, personal sponsorships and other commercial income.

The strangest part of the Formula 1 salary ladder is what happens immediately before a driver reaches it. In Formula 2, most drivers are not being paid millions. They are usually paying millions to race.

A young driver’s financial journey can therefore go from needing €2–3 million to fund an F2 season to earning a seven-figure salary within a year of reaching Formula 1. At the other end of the grid, established World Champions command tens of millions of dollars annually.

So how much do F1 drivers actually make, how much do reserve drivers get paid, and why is the salary figure you see online not the amount a driver ultimately keeps?

How much do F1 drivers make?

There is an important caveat before looking at any F1 salary table: Formula 1 driver contracts are private. Teams do not publish what they pay their drivers, so every figure reported in the media is an estimate assembled from teams, managers and paddock sources.

That puts the estimated average across the grid at about $15.9 million and the median at around $10 million. Even the median misleads, because Formula 1 operates as a superstar economy rather than a normal profession. A few exceptionally valuable drivers earn enormously more than everyone below them.

The strange economics of getting there

The financial path to Formula 1 is unusual because drivers generally spend years paying to compete before anybody pays them. At junior level the funding comes from family money, personal sponsors, national motorsport programmes or F1 driver academies.

Formula 2 is the clearest example. Silverstone’s guide to the championship notes that F2 drivers are generally not paid to compete; their financial backing is a major part of securing a seat at all. Motorsport.com estimates that a full F2 programme typically requires a driver budget of roughly €2–3 million, and more with a leading team.

For years a driver is effectively raising money to keep racing. Then, if they reach Formula 1 and prove themselves, the seat becomes something a team is willing to pay millions of dollars for.

How much does a rookie earn?

There is no official rookie salary, but the lower end of the grid gives a reasonable indication. Estimates put Arvid Lindblad at roughly $500,000 to $1 million, Oliver Bearman and Liam Lawson at around $1 million, and Kimi Antonelli and Gabriel Bortoleto at around $2 million.

For almost anybody else, earning $1 million in your early twenties would be extraordinary. Within Formula 1 it means the team has an inexpensive driver.

A rookie has little negotiating leverage. Other highly rated young drivers are waiting for an opportunity, and the team does not yet know whether this one will develop into a race winner. That can change quickly. A young driver who starts beating an established teammate, scoring podiums and attracting interest from rival teams may find their next contract worth several times the first.

Do reserve drivers get paid?

Yes, but this is one of the least transparent areas of Formula 1 compensation, and there is no standard reserve driver salary.

The role means different things to different drivers. A reserve might be a young academy prospect waiting for an opportunity, an F2 graduate doing simulator work, or an experienced former Grand Prix driver providing a safe pair of hands if a race driver becomes unavailable. They can perform extensive simulator work, analyse data, help with car development, take part in testing and occasionally drive in FP1, and they must be ready to race at very short notice.

Reliable salary information is scarce enough that precise figures published online should be treated cautiously. One unusually public example emerged from the legal dispute involving Alex Palou and McLaren: an English High Court judgment records a $400,000 signing-on bonus connected to Palou agreeing to provide F1 reserve-driver services. That was not his entire package, but it is a rare glimpse of what simply securing a reserve driver’s services can be worth.

There is no fixed amount. Young reserves may receive modest retainers; experienced former F1 drivers can negotiate substantially more.

For many young drivers the salary is not the point of the job. Being one phone call away from an F1 seat is.

Why do some drivers earn fifty times more than others?

The gap between a rookie on $1 million and Verstappen’s estimated $70 million is fundamentally about leverage.

If a team loses an inexperienced rookie, several Formula 2 graduates or reserve drivers could take the seat. If Red Bull loses Max Verstappen, there is no queue of interchangeable four-time World Champions waiting to replace him.

Teams pay not merely for speed, but for scarcity.

A highly valued driver combines outright pace, consistency, technical feedback, tyre management, experience, commercial value and the ability to turn a competitive car into victories. The harder somebody is to replace, the stronger their position becomes.

F1 salary by age

Looking at a single season creates distortions. If the only 41-year-old on the current grid happens to be Lewis Hamilton, the average salary of a 41-year-old tells you almost nothing about what a normal driver earns at 41.

What the shape tells you

What matters is not whether a 26-year-old historically earned exactly $8.5 million, but the shape of the curve.

Drivers arrive cheaply. Between 18 and 21 the curve sits around $1–2 million in today’s money, while teams are still evaluating them and the drivers have little leverage.

Then it accelerates. By the mid-twenties a driver who has survived several seasons is no longer a prospect: the paddock knows whether they are competitive, whether they beat teammates and whether other teams want them. The curve rises from roughly $3 million at 22 to more than $10 million at 27.

The late twenties and early thirties are where many drivers reach maximum negotiating strength, with enough experience behind them and enough competitive years still ahead. That is when second, third and fourth major contracts become extremely valuable.

Why the curve does not fall with age

At first glance the data produces a strange result: the estimate for a 40-year-old is higher than for a 30-year-old. That does not mean teams value 40-year-olds more. It is survivorship bias.

Ordinary drivers rarely last until 40, and the ones who do are exceptional. Fernando Alonso has continued into his mid-forties because he is a multiple World Champion who remains valuable. Lewis Hamilton remains enormously valuable in his forties because of seven championships, more than a hundred victories and huge commercial recognition. The average driver who loses their edge at 34 simply disappears from the dataset.

So the statistically meaningful part of the curve is roughly 18 to 35, where there are many driver-seasons to compare. Beyond that, salary increasingly reflects who survived rather than what the age is worth.

Why the salary is not what a driver earns

When an article says a driver earns $30 million, that number usually represents an estimated team salary or retainer. It is not the driver’s total annual income, and it is certainly not what is left afterwards.

Bonuses can make it much larger

Contracts can contain bonuses tied to points, podiums, race victories, the Constructors’ Championship or the Drivers’ Championship. RacingNews365 estimates Lewis Hamilton’s Ferrari team salary at around $60 million while noting that bonuses and sponsorship can push his broader annual earnings toward $100 million. Two drivers on identical base salaries can finish a season with very different compensation.

Sponsorship can be worth millions more

The biggest drivers are global brands in their own right, with personal commercial relationships involving watches, clothing, drinks and technology companies. Those deals are separate from the money the team pays, and for a globally recognised champion that income can be large enough that ranking drivers by team salary alone gives an incomplete picture.

Then there are taxes

Gross income and take-home income are not the same thing. Many drivers have historically chosen to live in Monaco, which generally does not impose personal income tax on residents, although important exceptions exist, particularly for French nationals.

That does not make a driver’s worldwide earnings tax-free. Drivers compete and conduct commercial activity in many countries, and residence, nationality, where income is generated, treaty arrangements and the structure of sponsorship deals all affect what is owed. Without seeing an individual’s arrangements it is impossible to say what share of an estimated $30 million salary reaches them personally.

Drivers also run small businesses

An elite driver may employ or pay managers, agents, lawyers, accountants, trainers, physiotherapists and commercial advisers. Some services come from the team; others are part of the driver’s own operation. Salary, total earnings and take-home income are three different numbers.

Do driver salaries count toward the cost cap?

No, and this is part of why superstar salaries can grow so large. Under the FIA’s financial regulations, payments made to drivers for their driving services are explicitly excluded when a team’s Relevant Costs are calculated, along with travel and accommodation for those drivers.

Consider the alternative. If paying a driver $50 million meant giving up $50 million of aerodynamics and car development, teams would think about driver contracts very differently. Instead a team can spend heavily to secure an exceptional driver without a dollar-for-dollar hit to its regulated development budget.

Does the World Champion automatically earn the most?

No. There is no official salary scale in Formula 1. Winning a championship dramatically increases negotiating power, but contracts are signed at different times and in very different circumstances.

A driver’s salary depends on their existing contract, competing offers, the team’s resources, how badly the team wants to keep them, and what they were worth when the deal was signed. A young driver can become one of the best performers in Formula 1 while still racing under a comparatively cheap contract negotiated before their value was obvious. The interesting moment comes when that contract expires.

How quickly can a salary change?

Very quickly, in both directions.

Imagine a 21-year-old starting on $1 million who consistently beats their teammate, then scores a podium, and then a rival team asks about their contract. Their current team is no longer negotiating against another promising junior; it is negotiating against another Formula 1 team that wants the same driver. That is how leverage is created.

The reverse happens just as fast. A highly paid driver can struggle for two seasons, lose their seat, and find the next contract worth dramatically less, or find there is no race seat at all. F1 careers are short enough that when a driver signs can matter almost as much as the number itself.

From paying €3 million to earning $70 million

The clearest way to understand Formula 1 salaries is to look at the whole career. A teenager spends enormous sums racing karts. They then need funding for Formula 4 and Formula 3. If they reach Formula 2 they might need another €2–3 million simply to compete.

Then it reverses. Reach Formula 1 and the team might pay you $1 million. Prove yourself and the next contract might be worth $5 million. Become a consistent race winner and it could be $15 million or $30 million. Become one of the defining drivers of your generation and the current market suggests a team may value your services at $60–70 million a year before bonuses and endorsements.

Few careers contain such an extreme financial reversal, and as with everything else in Formula 1, performance is only part of it. Timing, available seats, contracts, teammates and the strength of the car all matter, and sometimes the biggest financial decision of a career is knowing when to leave one team for another.

F1 driver salary FAQ

How much is the lowest-paid F1 driver paid?
Published 2026 estimates put the lowest full-time salaries at roughly $500,000 to $1 million per season.
Who is the highest-paid F1 driver?
Max Verstappen is estimated at approximately $70 million in team salary, followed by Lewis Hamilton at around $60 million. These are media estimates rather than disclosed contracts.
Do F1 reserve drivers get paid?
Yes, but there is no published standard salary. Packages vary with experience, simulator and testing responsibilities, other racing commitments, and whether the driver is an established former F1 racer or a young prospect.
Do Formula 2 drivers get paid?
Usually not by the F2 team. Drivers commonly fund their seats through sponsors, family backing or F1 academies, with a competitive full season estimated at roughly €2–3 million.
Do F1 drivers get bonuses for winning?
Many contracts contain performance-related bonuses, though the terms are private. Reported salary estimates generally exclude them, so a successful driver’s real compensation can substantially exceed the advertised base.
Do F1 driver salaries count toward the cost cap?
No. Under the FIA’s financial regulations, compensation paid for driver services is excluded from the calculation of Relevant Costs.
