How to Become an F1 Driver

The real road from karting to Formula 1, and why being fast is only part of it.

Becoming a Formula 1 driver is one of the hardest career paths in professional sport.

There is no college draft, no single qualifying tournament, and no guaranteed ladder where winning at one level automatically promotes you to the next. Instead, an aspiring F1 driver has to progress through years of increasingly competitive racing, build a reputation, secure enough financial backing to keep racing, earn the required FIA licence points, and eventually convince one of the Formula 1 teams that they deserve one of the very few seats available.

The familiar route looks something like this:

Karting → Formula 4 → Formula 3 → Formula 2 → Formula 1

But that simple line hides just how difficult the journey really is.

Step 1: Start with karting

Almost every modern Formula 1 career begins in karting.

Karting is not simply a miniature version of racing for children. It is where young drivers develop many of the skills they will use throughout their careers: braking technique, overtaking, defending, race starts, reading grip levels, adapting to changing conditions, and racing wheel-to-wheel against other talented drivers.

Formula 1 itself describes karting as the starting point for the journey to F1, while the FIA structures Formula 4 as the first major single-seater step for drivers graduating from karting.

Many future professionals begin very young, sometimes before the age of ten. That does not mean there is a formal rule saying you must start at five or seven years old. It does mean that, by the time the best young drivers reach their mid-teens, many already have years of competitive racing behind them.

At the higher levels, karting itself becomes extremely serious. Drivers travel internationally, work with professional teams and coaches, analyse data, test frequently and compete against many of the same people they may later meet again in Formula 3 or Formula 2.

The biggest barrier shows up well before any of that, though, and it is money.

Even before reaching cars, a competitive international karting campaign can cost hundreds of thousands of pounds. Formula 1 has previously cited budgets of up to £300,000 for a full competitive karting season.

Step 2: Move into Formula 4

For many drivers, Formula 4 is the first serious step from karts into single-seater racing cars.

F4 championships exist in a number of countries and regions and are designed specifically as an entry point to the FIA single-seater ladder. Drivers suddenly have to learn much more than simply going fast: aerodynamic grip, car setup, tyre management, data analysis, race engineering and the physical demands of a larger, faster car all become important.

This is also where a driver’s career starts to become much more visible to professional teams and F1 junior programmes.

A strong F4 season does not mean an F1 team will immediately call. But it can be the point where a driver starts attracting:

  • junior academy interest
  • sponsors
  • professional management
  • stronger teams
  • opportunities in Formula Regional or Formula 3

Under the current FIA Super Licence system, winning an eligible national Formula 4 championship can earn 12 Super Licence points, which become important at the end of the ladder rather than at the start of it.

Step 3: Prove yourself in Formula 3

Formula 3 is where the field becomes dramatically more selective.

The FIA describes F3 as the third tier of the single-seater pyramid and a key training ground for Formula 2 and ultimately Formula 1. The cars are standardised, so the championship is designed to put greater emphasis on driver performance rather than teams designing fundamentally different machinery.

By this stage, almost everyone on the grid was outstanding at a lower level. Being “fast” is no longer enough.

Drivers are judged on things such as:

  • qualifying pace
  • consistency
  • tyre management
  • racecraft
  • adaptability
  • technical feedback
  • mistakes and crashes
  • performance under pressure

Most of all, they are judged on how they perform against other highly rated young drivers in similar machinery.

A top-three finish in the current FIA F3 championship is worth 30, 25 or 20 Super Licence points, depending on championship position.

This is usually the stage where a driver must start looking like a genuine F1 prospect rather than simply a talented racer.

Step 4: Reach Formula 2

Formula 2 is the final conventional step before Formula 1.

It is deliberately designed to prepare drivers for the demands of F1. The cars are significantly faster and more powerful than F3 machinery, race weekends are held alongside Formula 1, and the paddock is filled with F1 team personnel watching potential future drivers.

A top-three championship finish in F2 is worth 40 Super Licence points, which on its own satisfies the main points requirement for an F1 Super Licence.

But even winning Formula 2 does not guarantee an F1 seat.

Formula 2 is not a promotion league where the champion automatically moves up. There still has to be:

  1. an available Formula 1 seat,
  2. a team that wants you,
  3. the necessary licence eligibility,
  4. and often significant commercial or academy support.

Some F2 champions have reached F1 almost immediately. Others have had to wait. Some highly rated drivers have never received a full-time F1 opportunity at all.

There are simply fewer seats than talented drivers.

The FIA Super Licence: the legal barrier to entering F1

Even if a Formula 1 team wants to sign a driver, that driver normally cannot simply enter a Grand Prix. They need an FIA Formula One Driver Super Licence.

Under the FIA’s current rules, a first-time applicant generally must:

  • hold an FIA International Grade A licence,
  • normally be at least 18 at the start of their first F1 competition,
  • complete at least 80% of each of two eligible single-seater seasons,
  • pass a knowledge assessment covering the International Sporting Code and F1 Sporting Regulations,
  • and accumulate at least 40 Super Licence points from eligible championships.

There is now an exception allowing the FIA, at its discretion, to grant a Super Licence at age 17 to a driver who has demonstrated exceptional ability and maturity in single-seater competition.

Super Licence points are earned according to championship results. Under the current system:

Other championships such as IndyCar, Formula E, Super Formula and endurance racing can also contribute points. So the traditional F4 → F3 → F2 route is not the only theoretical route to eligibility, but it remains the clearest pathway for most young drivers targeting Formula 1.

You also need somebody to give you a seat

This is the part that statistics and licence rules cannot capture. There are only a tiny number of Formula 1 race seats.

Being eligible to race in F1 is therefore very different from actually becoming an F1 driver.

Teams consider much more than championship results. They may look at:

  • outright speed
  • age and development potential
  • consistency
  • technical feedback
  • simulator performance
  • marketability
  • relationship with the team’s junior programme
  • performance against teammates
  • available seats, contractual politics and timing

Timing can be enormous. A brilliant young driver can reach Formula 2 when every F1 team already has two drivers under contract. Another may arrive just as a team needs a rookie. A driver’s entire career can change because one team decides to rebuild, another veteran retires, or a junior academy unexpectedly has an opening.

That is why the road to F1 is not simply a test of driving ability. It is also a sequence of career decisions and opportunities.

Why F1 driver academies matter

Most Formula 1 teams operate some form of junior-driver programme. These programmes can provide young drivers with financial support, coaching, simulator access, introductions to stronger teams and, most importantly, a direct relationship with an F1 organisation.

But academy membership is not a guarantee. A junior programme can create opportunity, but it also creates pressure. If several drivers belong to the same academy, they are effectively competing with each other for a very small number of future seats.

A driver may therefore face career choices such as:

  • Join the prestigious academy, or remain independent?
  • Take a reserve role with a top team, or accept an immediate race seat elsewhere?
  • Move up a category quickly, or stay another year and try to win the championship?

Those decisions can matter almost as much as lap time.

How expensive is the road to Formula 1?

This is the least romantic part of the answer.

Estimates of the full journey from childhood karting to being ready for Formula 1 run to several million pounds, with Formula 3 budgets around £1.5 million and Formula 2 budgets above £2 million. Other estimates place a competitive F2 seat around €2–3 million per season, depending on the team.

These figures vary considerably by driver, team, testing programme, series and sponsorship arrangements, so they should not be read as fixed prices. But the underlying reality is clear: motorsport talent is expensive to demonstrate.

Drivers therefore rely on combinations of family funding, personal sponsors, national motorsport programmes, driver academies, commercial backers, scholarships and team support.

This financial barrier means that some drivers with enough talent may never progress far enough to prove it.

Does an F1 driver have to be physically fit?

An F1 driver may look stationary inside the cockpit, but the physical demands are extreme.

Drivers must withstand sustained lateral and longitudinal forces while simultaneously braking, steering, managing controls, communicating with engineers and making strategic decisions.

Physical preparation typically focuses heavily on cardiovascular fitness, neck strength, core strength, heat tolerance, reaction time, recovery, and maintaining a very precise body weight.

Mental performance is equally important. A junior driver may have only one or two qualifying laps to prove their pace, while knowing that millions of pounds and potentially their career depend on the result. The ability to make good decisions while exhausted and under pressure becomes increasingly important as drivers climb the ladder.

Do you have to start racing as a child?

There is no FIA rule saying that an F1 driver must have begun karting at a particular childhood age.

Practically, however, reaching Formula 1 after starting motorsport late would be extraordinarily difficult. The reason is not simply age. It is accumulated experience.

By age 16, an elite junior driver may already have completed hundreds of races and thousands of competitive laps. A newcomer of the same age is trying to learn basic racecraft while competing against drivers who have spent a decade doing it.

Starting late does not mean someone cannot enjoy motorsport or even become a professional racing driver. It does mean the specific objective of reaching Formula 1 becomes increasingly improbable.

Is talent enough?

No, and this is the part most often left out. An F1 driver needs extraordinary talent, but talent is only one part of the equation.

Imagine two equally quick 18-year-olds. One chooses the right team, develops well, attracts an academy, gets enough funding, avoids serious injuries and reaches F2 just as an F1 seat becomes available. The other joins the wrong team, has a difficult season, loses sponsorship and never gets another opportunity at the same level.

Their underlying ability might be almost identical. Their careers can still become completely different.

Motorsport careers turn on that, and it can be brutal.

The simplest version of the road to F1

  1. Start racing karts. Learn racecraft and compete seriously.
  2. Succeed at national and international karting level. Build experience, results and sponsorship.
  3. Move into Formula 4. Learn single-seater racing.
  4. Progress through Formula Regional and/or Formula 3. Compete against elite junior talent and earn Super Licence points.
  5. Reach Formula 2. Prove that you are ready for the final step.
  6. Accumulate at least 40 FIA Super Licence points and meet the other licence requirements.
  7. Convince an F1 team to give you one of its seats.

Number seven may be the hardest part of all.

So, how hard is it to become an F1 driver?

A future Formula 1 driver needs to be very fast, very young, very experienced, physically prepared, mentally resilient, financially backed, strategically smart, and lucky enough to meet the right opportunities at the right time.

Winning races matters. But so do the decisions between races.

Which championship do you enter? Which team do you sign with? Do you join an academy? Do you move up early? Do you accept a reserve role? Do you stay loyal to the team that developed you, or take a risky opportunity somewhere else?

There is no single perfect route. There is only a long series of decisions that eventually lead a very small number of drivers onto the Formula 1 grid.
